Bengal Cat Lifespan
& Age Guide.

With their wild-cat markings and boundless energy, Bengals are one of the most distinctive breeds around. They're active, athletic, and generally quite healthy. If you share your home with one — or you're thinking about it — here's what actually affects how long they tend to live, and what to keep an eye on as they age.

Average Lifespan

Most Bengals live between 12 and 16 years, and some well-cared-for cats live longer still. Estimates vary by source, and the breed's relatively recent development means there's less long-term data than for older breeds. What's clear is that Bengals tend to do well with an active lifestyle, a good diet, and regular veterinary care.

Why Bengals Age a Little Differently

The standard veterinary formula for converting to human-equivalent age applies to Bengals just as it does to any other cat. What sets the breed apart is its high energy and athleticism, which tend to continue well into a Bengal's senior years compared to more sedentary breeds. That same drive means a Bengal needs consistent mental and physical stimulation throughout life to avoid boredom-related stress, and owners often notice a real dip in energy as the clearest early sign of aging, more so than with calmer breeds.

What Affects a Bengal's Lifespan

  • Genetics and breeding quality: Responsible breeders screen for inherited heart conditions, which meaningfully lowers long-term health risk.
  • Indoor living: Bengals are curious and agile, which can put them at extra risk outdoors. Many owners keep them indoors or provide secure, enclosed outdoor access.
  • Exercise and enrichment: As a high-energy breed, Bengals benefit from regular play and mental stimulation, which supports both physical and behavioral health.
  • Regular veterinary care: Routine checkups catch the conditions below early, when they're most manageable.

Health Conditions to Watch For

Bengals are generally a robust, healthy breed, but like most purebred lines, they're associated with a few conditions worth knowing about:

  • Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM): A thickening of the heart muscle, seen across many breeds, and worth discussing with your vet as your cat ages.
  • Progressive Retinal Atrophy (PRA): An inherited eye condition that can lead to gradual vision loss. Genetic testing is available and used by many reputable breeders.
  • Patellar luxation: A kneecap condition that can affect mobility, worth mentioning to your vet if you notice occasional limping or an unusual gait.
  • Dental disease: Common in cats of all breeds, and worth staying ahead of with regular dental checkups.

None of this makes a Bengal a fragile cat. It simply means knowing what to ask your vet about, particularly as they move into their mature and senior years.
