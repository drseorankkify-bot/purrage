Cat behaviour

Cat Body
Language.

Cats communicate far more with their bodies than their voices. The tail, ears, eyes, whiskers and posture together show whether a cat is relaxed, stressed, afraid or possibly in pain. This guide explains the main signals and how to read them in context.

Body language is also one of the earliest ways to spot a problem, because cats often hide illness. For the wider picture, see our cat behaviour guide.

The tail

A tail held upright with a curled tip signals a happy, confident cat. Flicking from side to side while a cat is stalking a toy or target usually means hunting or play, but flicking when nothing is being stalked usually means annoyance. A tail held close to the body, thrashed or thumped points to stress, and a tail tucked under or wrapped tightly around the body can mean fear or pain.

The ears

Ears upright and facing forward suggest a happy or playful cat. When they rotate sideways or angle back, the cat is stressed, and the further back they go, the more afraid it is likely to be. Ears turned back or flat against the head signal aggression. Ears held low or rotated to the sides can be a sign of illness or pain, which is why a change in ear position is worth noticing in older cats.

The eyes

Round or partly closed eyes with narrowed pupils suggest contentment. Slow blinking means the cat trusts you, and slow-blinking back is a friendly response. Big eyes with dilated pupils can mean play, but wide eyes with dilated pupils can also mean fear. Squinted, shut, dazed or glassy eyes, sometimes with the third eyelid showing, can be a sign of illness or pain.

Whiskers and posture

Whiskers held further forward than normal suggest stress, whiskers pulled flat against the face suggest fear, and whiskers flat against the cheeks or pushed forward can signal aggression. A loose body with the limbs and tail stretched out means a relaxed cat. Showing the belly suggests the cat feels safe, though it is not always an invitation to rub it. A stiff, tense body held low means stress, a crouched and scrunched body means fear, and an arched back with raised fur is aggression or an attempt to look bigger.

Sounds that go with body language

Purring usually means contentment, but cats can also purr when they are in pain or ill (see why cats purr). Meowing often greets people or asks for food or attention, and more meowing or yowling than usual can signal emotional or physical distress (see why cats meow). Growling and hissing are warnings, and hissing is largely an involuntary reflex when a cat is scared.

Read the whole picture

  • Look at context. The same posture can mean different things depending on the sights, sounds and smells around the cat.
  • Look for several signals at once. One cue on its own is less reliable than a tail, ears and eyes all pointing the same way.
  • Don't pick up a growling or hissing cat. This can provoke aggression, which usually reflects fear or panic rather than malice.
  • Watch for stress behaviours such as out-of-place lip licking, yawning, grooming or scratching, or urinating outside the litter box.

When body language points to a health problem

A cat that seems upset for no clear reason may be sick or in pain. Persistent changes in hiding, aggression, energy, appetite, grooming or sociability warrant a vet visit. In older cats, new pain signals can come from conditions like arthritis or dental disease, and new confusion can be a sign of cognitive decline. Find a clinic with our cat vet finder.

Frequently asked questions

What does a slow blink from a cat mean?

A slow blink means the cat trusts you. Slow-blinking back at your cat is a friendly response.

What does it mean when a cat's tail is up?

A tail held upright with a curled tip signals a happy, confident cat.

What do flat ears mean in a cat?

Ears turned back or flat against the head signal aggression. Ears held low or rotated to the sides can instead be a sign of illness or pain.

Can body language show that a cat is in pain?

Yes. A tail tucked tightly under the body, low ears, squinted or glassy eyes and a hunched posture with the legs tucked under can all suggest pain or illness. Persistent changes should be checked by a vet.

— This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If your cat seems unwell, contact a vet.
