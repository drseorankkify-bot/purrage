Maine Coon Lifespan
& Age Guide.

Maine Coons are one of the largest domestic cat breeds, and one of the longest-lived too. If you share your home with one — or you're thinking about it — here's what actually affects how long they tend to live, and what to keep an eye on as they age.

Average Lifespan

Most Maine Coons live between 12 and 15 years, with many healthy individuals reaching 16 to 18 with good care. A few exceptional cases have lived into their late 20s or even 30s — the most famous being a Maine Coon named Creme Puff, who reportedly lived to 38, the longest-documented lifespan of any cat on record. Cases like that are rare outliers, not a realistic expectation, but they show just how much genetics and care can extend a healthy cat's life beyond the average.

Why Large Breeds Age a Little Differently

Maine Coons take longer to reach full physical maturity than most cats — often 3 to 5 years, compared to roughly 1 year for smaller breeds. That slower start doesn't mean their formula for converting to human-equivalent age is different; the standard veterinary formula still applies. What it does mean is that a Maine Coon may still look and act "young" well past the age other cats have settled into adulthood, and it's worth not mistaking that continued growth for anything unusual.

What Affects a Maine Coon's Lifespan

  • Genetics: Reputable breeders screen for hereditary conditions common in the breed, which meaningfully reduces long-term health risk.
  • Indoor vs. outdoor living: Indoor cats generally live longer, avoiding the risks of traffic, predators, and disease exposure.
  • Diet and weight management: Given their large frame, keeping a Maine Coon at a healthy weight matters more than it might for a smaller breed.
  • Regular veterinary care: Early detection of breed-specific conditions makes a real difference in long-term outcomes.

Health Conditions to Watch For

Maine Coons are a generally healthy breed, but like most purebred lines, they carry a predisposition toward a few specific conditions worth knowing about:

  • Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM): A thickening of the heart muscle, and the most commonly reported health concern in the breed. Regular veterinary checkups can catch this early.
  • Hip dysplasia: More common in large-bodied breeds generally, and worth mentioning to your vet given the Maine Coon's size.
  • Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA): A hereditary condition some breeders now screen for directly.

None of this means a Maine Coon is a "high-risk" pet — it means knowing what to ask your vet about, particularly as they move into their mature and senior years.
