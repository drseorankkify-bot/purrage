Cat behaviour

Why Do Cats
Knead?

Kneading, the rhythmic pushing of the paws, begins in kittenhood when kittens knead their mother to stimulate milk flow. Some cats carry the habit into adulthood. No one knows exactly why, but there are several well-supported ideas, and a few signs that kneading is worth a vet visit.

Kneading is the rhythmic pushing of a cat's front paws against a soft surface, often called "making biscuits". It is one of the most familiar cat behaviours, and one of the least certain to explain. This guide sets out the main theories, what to do when claws hurt, and when kneading is a reason to see a vet. For the wider picture, see our cat behaviour guide.

Where kneading comes from

Kneading starts with nursing. Kittens knead around their mother to stimulate the flow of milk, and are rewarded with milk. PetMD adds that nursing and the mother's calming pheromones promote contentment, which may be why many cats keep kneading as adults to feel calm and relaxed.

Why adult cats still knead

No one knows for certain why some adult cats keep kneading, as Cats Protection points out. The explanations below come from it and from PetMD, and several may apply at once.

Because cats rarely do something for no reason, kneading probably has a purpose even when we cannot see it. A cat kneading while purring and relaxed on your lap is most likely content. See why cats purr for what the sound can mean, and cat body language for the other signals to read alongside it.

It helps to remember that these ideas are not rivals. A cat can knead a blanket because the texture triggers the habit, because the habit feels calming, and because it leaves a little scent, all at the same time. That is why the same behaviour can look different from one cat to the next, and why the most useful clue is usually how relaxed your own cat looks while doing it.

Kneading and age

Kneading often starts in kittenhood. PetMD says some cats keep doing it throughout their lives, while others stop after kittenhood, and it does not explain why. The habit is not a sign of age, so a senior cat that kneads is no cause for concern on its own. If your cat starts kneading much more than usual at any age, see "When to ask a vet" below. If kneading hurts: protecting your lap

Kneading is natural and should not be punished. PetMD advises the following:

  1. Keep the claws short. Trim nails regularly, and get your cat used to trims early using treats so it is a positive experience. See our senior cat grooming guide for how to trim safely, which matters more as claws thicken with age.
  2. Use a barrier. Place a blanket over your legs or stomach.
  3. Redirect. Offer a thick blanket or a designated spot, and reward your cat when it uses it. Cats also tend to prefer beds off the ground.
  4. Never punish it. Do not yell, hit or use a squirt bottle. Punishment does not address the reason a cat kneads.
  5. Do not declaw. PetMD describes declawing as painful and says it can cause lameness and permanent nerve damage.

Kneading in kittens

In kittens, kneading is a normal part of nursing and development, and there is nothing to fix. Kittens that are used to being handled early tend to accept nail trims more easily, and PetMD recommends getting a cat used to trims early, with treats so it is a positive experience. Working out why your cat kneads

Since several explanations can apply, look at the situation rather than the paws alone. These questions can help:

  • Where is it happening? Kneading on a bed or blanket before lying down suggests settling in. Kneading on you may signal comfort and affection.
  • What does the rest of the body say? A loose, relaxed body points to contentment. Tense posture, hiding or restlessness points elsewhere; see cat body language.
  • Is it new or increasing? A long-standing habit is usually fine. A recent increase is the thing PetMD says to raise with a vet.
  • Has anything changed at home? A move, a new pet or a change in routine can affect anxious cats.
  • Is there a pattern with age? Some cats keep kneading all their lives and others stop after kittenhood, so a cat that does not knead is not missing anything.

Kneading and suckling

Some cats suckle on a blanket while kneading it. PetMD mentions this as part of the comfort-seeking behaviour that goes back to nursing. If your cat sucks fabric often or eats it, mention that to your vet, since a vet can tell whether it is harmless comfort behaviour or something to manage.

When to ask a vet

PetMD says kneading is usually normal and not a cause for worry, but increased or excessive kneading may indicate discomfort, anxiety or a compulsive behavioural disorder. Pain from an injury or arthritis may also prompt kneading. If kneading increases or seems new, consult your vet. Depending on the cause, options for stress or anxiety can include medication, supplements or pheromone diffusers, and your vet can advise.

In older cats, any new or increased behaviour is worth noting, because arthritis and other conditions often show up as changes in habits before anything else. Find a clinic with our cat vet finder, and see our senior cat health guide for the wider list of signs.

Frequently asked questions

Why do cats knead blankets and people?

Kneading begins in kittenhood, when kittens knead their mother to stimulate milk flow. Adult cats may continue because it feels calming, because they are preparing a place to rest, to leave scent from the glands in their paw pads, or from habit. No one knows for certain.

Does kneading mean a cat is happy?

It often goes with contentment, and PetMD says it can signal comfort, security and affection. But no single meaning is confirmed, and increased or new kneading can also point to discomfort or anxiety.

Why does my cat knead and purr at the same time?

Both are commonly linked to comfort and calm. Purring is not always a sign of happiness, though, since cats can also purr when stressed or in pain, so look at the rest of your cat's body language.

How do I stop my cat's claws hurting when it kneads?

Keep the claws trimmed, put a blanket over your lap as a barrier, and redirect your cat to a thick blanket or a designated spot, rewarding it when it uses it. Never punish kneading, and do not declaw.

Do all cats knead?

No. PetMD says some cats keep kneading throughout life while others stop after kittenhood.

Is excessive kneading a problem?

It can be. PetMD says increased or excessive kneading may indicate discomfort, anxiety or a compulsive behavioural disorder, and pain from an injury or arthritis may also prompt it. Consult your vet if it increases or seems new.
