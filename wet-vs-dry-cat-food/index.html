Feeding guide

Wet vs Dry
Cat Food.

Both can feed a cat well. The real question is which suits your cat's age, health and habits. This guide compares them side by side and shows when a mix makes sense.

Quick comparison

FactorWet foodDry food
Water contentHigh: canned food can be up to about 85% waterLow: about 6 to 10% water
CaloriesLess energy dense, so a bigger volume per calorieMore calorie dense, easy to overfeed
Easy to chewYes, good for kittens, seniors and sore mouthsHarder for cats with dental problems
ConvenienceOpened cans need the fridge, messierEasy to measure, store and leave out
CostUsually more per servingUsually cheaper

The water figures come from Catster's feeding guide, and the pros and cons from Banfield Pet Hospital.

Why hydration matters

Cats do not always drink much. Texas A&M's Dr. Audrey Cook says wet food, with its extra moisture, helps cats that do not drink enough and is preferred for cats with kidney disease or bladder stones. It also helps urinary health. See kidney disease in cats.

Weight and calories

Wet food is less energy dense, so it can help cats that need to limit calories by keeping them fuller. Dry food packs more calories into the same volume, and a bowl that is always full makes overeating easy. Measure either type. See how much to feed a cat and healthy cat weight.

Teeth

Dry food is linked with less plaque, but it does not replace brushing or dental checks. Wet food is easier to eat for cats with sore teeth. See cat dental disease.

Mixing wet and dry

Banfield calls mixing a popular middle ground, for example wet food in the morning and dry at night, or wet food as a topper on kibble. Texas A&M's Dr. Cook describes feeding wet food several times a day with a small measured amount of dry food for snacking. When you mix, split the daily calories between the two instead of feeding full portions of each.

What matters more than the format

  • Quality: choose a well-established, trustworthy brand. Quality varies widely with ingredients and formulation.
  • Complete and balanced: vets look for AAFCO logos and a named protein, like chicken or salmon, as the first ingredient, with limited fillers and artificial additives.
  • Life stage: match the food to kitten, adult or senior needs.
  • Variety: Dr. Cook recommends feeding various flavours and brands from kittenhood so cats learn to accept a range of foods.

Wet food safety

Wet food should not be left out for hours because it is not hygienic or palatable. Store opened cans in the fridge, and discard food that has been sitting out.

If your cat will not eat

Dr. Cook's advice is that "not hungry" suggests a medical issue, while "not liking a food" is simply a preference. If a cat consistently refuses food, see a vet first. To change foods safely, read how to switch cat food. For the full overview, see the cat feeding guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is wet food better than dry food for cats?

Neither is better for every cat. Vets say it depends on the cat. Wet food helps hydration, dry is convenient, and many cats do well on both.

Can cats eat only dry food?

Yes, but dry food has little moisture, so make sure your cat drinks well and consider adding wet food, especially for seniors or cats with urinary or kidney concerns.

Is wet food bad for teeth?

Not on its own. Dental disease depends on plaque and care. Daily brushing and vet checks matter more than the food format.

How much wet food equals dry food?

There is no fixed ratio. Compare calories per can or cup on the labels and divide your cat's daily calories between them.
