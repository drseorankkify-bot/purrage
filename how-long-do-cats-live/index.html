The bigger picture

How Long Do
Cats Live?

It's one of the first questions anyone asks when they bring a cat home. The honest answer is "it depends" — but research gives us a pretty clear picture of what it depends on, and how much is actually within an owner's control.

The Average Cat Lifespan

Most sources place the average cat's lifespan somewhere between 12 and 18 years, with plenty of indoor cats living comfortably into their late teens or twenties. The range is wide because so many individual factors — indoor versus outdoor living, spaying or neutering, breed, weight, and veterinary care — each shift the number meaningfully in either direction.

Indoor vs. Outdoor: The Single Biggest Factor

Nothing affects a cat's lifespan more than whether they live indoors. Indoor cats typically live somewhere in the 12 to 18 year range, while outdoor-only cats average just 2 to 5 years, largely due to traffic, predators, disease exposure, and fights with other animals. Interestingly, one large necropsy study from UC Davis found that indoor-outdoor cats, meaning indoor cats with occasional supervised outdoor access, didn't show a significantly shorter lifespan than indoor-only cats. It was unrestricted outdoor-only living specifically that cut life expectancy short. If your cat currently has outdoor access, moving toward an indoor or supervised-outdoor setup is one of the most effective things you can do for their long-term health.

Does Spaying or Neutering Really Matter?

The data here is striking. A large necropsy study published in PLOS ONE, examining over 3,100 cats, found that spayed females had a median age at death of 10.5 years, compared to 4.7 years for intact females over one year old. Neutered males showed a similar pattern: a median of 9.8 years, compared to 3.7 years for intact males. The gap is partly explained by reduced roaming and fighting, and partly by a lower risk of certain reproductive cancers and infections. Spaying or neutering is one of the few single decisions an owner can make with this much documented impact on lifespan.

Purebred vs. Mixed-Breed Cats

A 2015 UK study on cat mortality, covering around 4,000 cats, found that crossbred cats had a somewhat higher median lifespan than purebred cats — 14 years compared to 12.5 years. This doesn't mean any individual purebred cat is destined for a shorter life; it reflects that a wider gene pool generally carries fewer concentrated inherited conditions. Some purebred lines, like Siamese, Russian Blue, and American Shorthair, are themselves reported among the longer-lived breeds, so breed-specific research matters more than a simple purebred-versus-mixed split.

Does Breed Actually Change the Number?

Yes, though usually by a few years rather than dramatically. Breed affects lifespan mainly through inherited health conditions, body size, and coat type, rather than aging at a fundamentally different pace. We've covered the lifespan and health profile of individual breeds in detail — see all our breed guides, or the longest-living cat breeds if you want the short version.

What Actually Moves the Number

  • Keeping your cat indoors, or giving them only supervised outdoor access, is the single biggest factor within an owner's control.
  • Spaying or neutering is linked to a significantly longer median lifespan in large studies.
  • A healthy weight reduces the risk of diabetes, joint problems, and heart strain as cats age.
  • Regular veterinary checkups catch common age-related conditions, like kidney and dental disease, while they're still manageable.
