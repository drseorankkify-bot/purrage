The bigger picture

How Long Do
Cats Live?

Most pet cats live somewhere between about 12 and 16 years, and many reach their late teens. The exact figure depends on which group of cats is measured, and on factors such as neutering, weight, breed and safety. This guide sets out what two large studies found and which factors an owner can influence.

This page answers the question in layers: the typical numbers, what two large studies found, what moves the number up or down, the record-holders, and what you can do. For a cat's age in human terms, see how cats age or use the PurrAge calculator.

The figures disagree because they measure different groups. The English study sampled cats from ordinary vet practices, so its median is the closest we have to a typical pet cat. The UC Davis study only includes cats that were examined after death at a teaching hospital, which the authors say may favour a less healthy group with a shorter life. A fair summary is that most pet cats live somewhere between about 12 and 16 years, and cats that are well cared for and kept safe often live longer.

What the English study found

The VetCompass study, published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery in 2015, looked at 4,009 cats that had died, drawn at random from 118,016 cats attending 90 practices in England. Its main results were:

  • Median age at death was 14.0 years.
  • Crossbred cats lived longer than purebred cats: a median of 14.0 years compared with 12.5 years. Individual breeds varied substantially.
  • Neutered cats lived longer than entire (unneutered) cats among those that died at 5 years or older.
  • Lower body weight went with longer life in cats that died at 5 years or older.

A summary of the same study by the veterinary blog SkeptVet reports two peaks in deaths, one at about 1 year of age and one at about 16. It also notes that trauma was the leading cause of death in cats under 5, at 47%, and that kidney disease was the leading cause in cats over 5, at 13.6%. The same summary cautions that causes of death were not independently verified, and that the neutering link may be partly explained by differences in outdoor access. Treat these as indications rather than exact figures.

What the UC Davis necropsy study found

Kent and colleagues published their study in PLOS ONE in 2022. It covered 3,108 client-owned cats examined after death between 1989 and 2019, of which 84.2% were mixed breed. For cats that lived past their first birthday, median age at death was:

Group (cats over 1 year)Median age at death
Spayed females10.48 years
Intact (unspayed) females4.68 years
Neutered males9.84 years
Intact (unneutered) males3.67 years

The authors state that intact cats had significantly shorter lifespans than spayed or neutered cats. Because these are ages at death within a referral hospital population, they should not be read as the lifespan of a typical cat. The gap is still large, and it is partly explained by roaming, fighting and reproductive disease, though the study cannot separate those causes. The authors also note that age at spay and litter history were not recorded for most female cats.

Indoor, indoor-outdoor and outdoor cats

Housing is the most commonly quoted factor, but the evidence is more nuanced than a single number. In the UC Davis study, considering all cats, median age at death was:

HousingMedian age at death (all cats)Cats over 1 year
Indoor only9.43 years9.98 years
Indoor-outdoor9.82 years10.09 years
Outdoor only7.25 years9.80 years

For all cats, outdoor-only cats had significantly shorter lives than the other two groups. Once cats under a year old were removed, the differences were not statistically significant. Only 190 cats in the study were outdoor-only, so the numbers are small. Other sources quote much larger gaps: Great Pet Care puts outdoor cats at an average of about 5.6 years, and Purina lists theft, getting lost, road injuries, poisonous plants and fights among the risks. The honest reading is that outdoor living adds real risks, particularly early in life, but a precise number for how many years it costs is not settled. A supervised garden, a catio or a harness are lower-risk ways to give a cat outdoor time.

Why breed matters

Breed changes the number mainly through inherited health conditions, body size and coat type, rather than by making a cat age at a different pace. A 2024 study quoted by Purina found the Burmese had the longest average in the UK at 14.4 years and the Sphynx the shortest at 6.8 years. We cover each breed in detail on the all cat breeds page, or see the longest-living cat breeds for the short version. Examples include Siamese, Burmese and Sphynx.

The oldest cats on record

Guinness World Records lists Creme Puff, owned by Jake Perry of Austin, Texas, as the oldest cat ever. She was born on 3 August 1967 and died on 6 August 2005, aged 38 years and 3 days. That is far beyond the normal range, and no study suggests it is a realistic target, but it shows how long a cat can live in good conditions. More on the record-holders is on our oldest cat ever page.

Lifespan and life stages

A cat's care needs change as it ages. Kittens, adults and seniors face different risks, and vets generally treat a cat as senior from about 10. Understanding where your cat is helps you plan checkups and spot problems early.

  • When is a cat senior? explains the 10-year line.
  • Senior cat health and senior cat care cover the conditions and routines that matter most in later life, including kidney disease and dental disease.

How to help your cat live longer

  1. Spay or neuter your cat, ideally after talking with your vet about timing.
  2. Keep your cat at a healthy weight. Weigh regularly and adjust portions.
  3. Reduce outdoor risks. Keep cats in at night, and use supervised or enclosed outdoor space.
  4. Keep up vaccinations and vet checks. Purina lists vaccinations, regular checks and microchipping among its tips for longer lives.
  5. Feed a balanced diet suited to your cat's age. See the senior cat diet guide.
  6. Provide enrichment and exercise, such as climbing, hunting-style play and stress-free access to food, water and the litter box.
  7. Know the warning signs and find a clinic with our cat vet finder.

Frequently asked questions

How long do indoor cats live?

In the English VetCompass study, the median age at death for all cats was 14.0 years. Sources quote different averages for indoor cats, from about 12 to about 17 years, because they measure different groups of cats.

How long do outdoor cats live?

Estimates vary. One veterinary source puts the average at about 5.6 years, while a UC Davis necropsy study found a median age at death of 7.25 years for outdoor-only cats, with no significant difference from indoor cats once cats under 1 year were excluded. Outdoor living adds risks such as road injuries and fights.

Do spayed and neutered cats live longer?

Yes. Both the English and UC Davis studies found that neutered cats lived longer. In the UC Davis study, spayed females had a median age at death of 10.48 years compared with 4.68 for intact females, and neutered males 9.84 compared with 3.67 for intact males.

Do purebred cats live shorter lives than crossbred cats?

On average, yes. The English study found a median of 14.0 years for crossbred cats and 12.5 years for purebred cats, though individual breeds vary substantially.

What is the oldest a cat has ever lived?

Guinness World Records lists Creme Puff, who lived 38 years and 3 days (3 August 1967 to 6 August 2005), as the oldest cat ever.

At what age is a cat considered old?

Vets generally consider a cat senior from about 10 years old. See our guide to when a cat is senior for details.
