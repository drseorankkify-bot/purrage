Cat behaviour

Why Do Cats
Purr?

Cats purr for several reasons. Contentment is the best known, but purring is also used to bond, to ask for things, to calm down and sometimes when a cat is in pain or under stress. The context tells you which one you are hearing.

Purring is one of many cat signals. Read it together with the rest of your cat's body language, and see our cat behaviour guide for the wider picture.

The main reasons cats purr

ReasonWhat is going on
ContentmentA relaxed cat, often curled up, purrs when it feels safe and comfortable
BondingMothers use soft vibrations to tell kittens it is safe to approach and nurse, and kittens purr back
CommunicationCats may purr to ask for food or attention, to signal a wish to play, or to show they are not a threat to other cats
Self-soothingPurring can calm a cat in a new environment or around unfamiliar animals, much as people hum when anxious
HealingResearch suggests purring may support healing, though this is still being studied
Pain or illnessCats may purr when they are in pain or discomfort

Purring to ask for something

AAHA describes a “solicitation purr”, which includes a high-frequency element similar to a baby's cry. Cats may use it to ask for food or attention. If your cat purrs insistently around mealtimes, this may be what you are hearing.

Purring when in pain or stressed

Purring is not always a sign of happiness. Cats may purr when in pain or discomfort, and may also purr in stressful situations. One theory is that the vibrations may release endorphins, the body's natural pain relievers, but this is presented as a theory rather than a proven fact. This is why purring alone is not proof that a cat is well.

What to look at besides the purr

  • Body posture: a loose, stretched-out cat is relaxed, while a hunched cat with its legs tucked under may be in pain (see cat body language).
  • Appetite and energy: a purring cat that is also eating less, hiding or moving stiffly needs a closer look.
  • Setting: purring at a vet clinic or in a new place is more likely to be self-soothing than relaxation.

When to call the vet

AAHA advises contacting a vet if purring seems unusual or comes with other behaviour changes, so they can work out whether it reflects comfort, stress or illness. This matters more in older cats, who are prone to problems like arthritis and kidney disease. See our senior cat health guide for warning signs.

Frequently asked questions

Do cats only purr when they are happy?

No. Cats purr when they are content, but also to communicate, to calm themselves, and sometimes when they are in pain or stressed.

Why do kittens purr?

Mother cats use soft vibrations to tell kittens it is safe to approach and nurse, and kittens purr back.

Can purring heal a cat?

AAHA says research suggests purring may support healing, but this is an area of ongoing study and purring is not a substitute for veterinary care.

When is purring a reason to call the vet?

If the purring seems unusual or comes with changes in behaviour, such as hiding, eating less or moving stiffly, contact a vet to find out whether it reflects comfort, stress or illness.

— This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If your cat seems unwell, contact a vet.
