10 Longest-Living
Cat Breeds.

Breed isn't the biggest factor in how long a cat lives — indoor living and spaying or neutering matter more — but it does shift the odds. Here are ten breeds commonly reported to live the longest, ranked loosely by the upper end of their typical range, with a link to the full guide for each.

1. Russian Blue — 15 to 20 years

One of the healthiest purebred lines around, with relatively few inherited conditions and a naturally reserved, low-drama temperament. Read the full Russian Blue guide →

2. American Shorthair — 15 to 20 years

A sturdy, broad-gene-pool breed with few breed-specific health issues, which goes a long way toward its reputation for longevity. Read the full American Shorthair guide →

3. Turkish Angora — 15 to 20 years

A naturally occurring breed with centuries of development behind it, generally healthy aside from a few specific inherited conditions. Read the full Turkish Angora guide →

4. Siamese — 12 to 20 years

Siamese cats are consistently reported among the longest-lived breeds, with many comfortably reaching the higher end of that range. Read the full Siamese guide →

5. Domestic Shorthair — 12 to 18 years

Not a formal breed, but the most common type of cat — and its wide genetic diversity tends to work in its favor. Read the full Domestic Shorthair guide →

6. Ragdoll — 12 to 17 years

A large, docile breed that does well with attentive care, despite a few breed-specific conditions to watch for. Read the full Ragdoll guide →

7. British Shorthair — 12 to 17 years

Calm, sturdy, and generally low-risk, though prone to weight gain given its laid-back nature. Read the full British Shorthair guide →

8. Birman — 12 to 16 years

A gentle, people-focused breed with relatively few breed-specific health conditions compared to many other purebred lines. Read the full Birman guide →

9. Norwegian Forest Cat — 12 to 16 years

A large, cold-hardy breed with a relatively unmanipulated gene pool and slow, multi-year growth. Read the full Norwegian Forest Cat guide →

10. Burmese — 10 to 16 years

Playful and people-oriented well into their senior years, with good care offsetting a somewhat narrower gene pool. Read the full Burmese guide →

What These Breeds Have in Common

A few patterns repeat across this list: a broad genetic background with fewer concentrated inherited conditions, a build that doesn't predispose to joint or breathing problems, and — just as importantly — owners who keep them indoors, manage their weight, and stay on top of routine vet care. Breed sets the range; daily care decides where in that range an individual cat lands.
