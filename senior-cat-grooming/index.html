Senior cat care

Senior Cat
Grooming.

Older cats often groom less, and a rough, greasy or matted coat is one of the first things owners notice. Senior cat grooming means helping with gentle brushing and regular nail trims, and treating a change in coat as a possible health clue, because pain and illness are common reasons a cat stops grooming.

This guide is part of our senior cat care guide. If your cat's coat has changed suddenly, read to the end for when to see a vet.

Why senior cats groom less

PetMD lists several reasons:

  • Arthritis. Many cats develop arthritis in the spine and hips, which can make the twisting motion of grooming painful and put some spots out of reach. See arthritis signs in cats.
  • Changes in the skin and coat. Skin oil production increases with age, which can cause mats even in short-haired cats. Brittle hair can also tangle.
  • Extra weight. Obesity makes grooming harder and can lead to dandruff, an unkempt coat and a build-up of urine or stool on the fur. See our guide to healthy cat weight.
  • Illness. Kidney disease, thyroid disorders and diabetes can reduce grooming too.

The American Association of Feline Practitioners also lists a poor coat and decreased grooming among the general signs of disease in older cats, so a change is worth raising at the next visit.

Brushing

  • PetMD says daily brushing is ideal for medium- and long-haired cats, and regular brushing helps all cats.
  • Use a gentle touch and a brush with soft bristles rather than wire.
  • Keep sessions short and pleasant, with praise or treats. Short, frequent sessions reduce stress for both of you.
  • Brush where your cat lets you. If a spot is painful, stop and tell your vet.

Nail care

PetMD notes that claws thicken with age and are shed less, so nails may need trimming more often to prevent painful ingrown claws. It recommends trimming monthly with cat nail clippers. Look at the nails when you cuddle your cat, and ask your vet to show you the right way if you are unsure.

Mats

Mats will not go away by themselves. PetMD advises against trying to brush out mats yourself, because older cats have delicate skin and cutting too close or using poor technique can cause cuts or skin damage. Take your cat to a groomer or vet to have them removed safely, and tell them your cat is older.

Use grooming as a quick health check

Brushing is a good time to look and feel over your cat. The AAFP lists these among the signs of disease to watch for in older cats:

A new lump, swelling or skin massBook a vet visit and note the size and where it is
A sore that does not heal, bleeding or dischargeBook a vet visit
A dull, greasy or unkempt coatMention it at the next visit, or sooner if there are other changes

PetMD recommends vet exams about every six months for senior cats, including bloodwork and blood pressure checks. If you do not have a clinic yet, use our cat vet finder.

Make grooming easier

Place the cat on a warm, non-slip surface at floor level, so they do not need to jump. Our senior cat home setup checklist covers other comfort changes.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my old cat not grooming?

Arthritis pain, extra weight and illnesses such as kidney disease, thyroid disorders or diabetes can all reduce grooming. A vet can check which applies.

How often should I brush a senior cat?

PetMD says daily brushing is ideal for medium- and long-haired cats. Short, gentle, regular sessions work best for any cat.

How often should I trim a senior cat's nails?

PetMD recommends about monthly. Claws thicken with age and shed less, so overgrown nails can become painful.

Can I cut mats out myself?

PetMD advises against it. Older cats have delicate skin, so ask a groomer or vet to remove mats safely.

Is a greasy or unkempt coat a sign of illness?

It can be. A poor coat and reduced grooming are listed among the signs of disease in older cats, so mention it to your vet.

— This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If your cat seems unwell or in pain, contact a vet.
