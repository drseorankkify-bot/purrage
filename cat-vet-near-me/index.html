What makes a clinic cat-friendly?

Cats are not small dogs, and a noisy waiting room full of barking can stress them before the exam even starts. The American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) runs the Cat Friendly Practice programme for clinics that take extra steps for cats: gentle handling, a feline-friendly environment and equipment suited to cats. The AAFP has a search tool on its website for finding one, and you can search by country, state or province. A clinic does not need the badge to be good, but it is a useful sign.

In the United States, the American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) accredits practices that pass regular on-site evaluations against its standards, and it also has a hospital finder. Accreditation is separate from being cat-friendly, so you can check both.

Look for these signs on a visit or phone call:

  • A separate waiting area, or a quiet corner where cats can sit away from dogs.
  • Staff who let you keep your cat in the carrier until the exam room is ready.
  • An unhurried exam, with gentle handling and a willingness to explain what they are doing.
  • Clear opening hours and a plan for emergencies outside those hours.

Questions to ask before you book

  • Do you see many cats, and do you have a separate or quiet area for them?
  • Can I stay with my cat during the exam?
  • What happens if my cat needs help when you are closed?
  • Do you offer senior wellness exams, including blood and urine tests and blood pressure checks?
  • How do you handle pain, and can I get a written treatment plan with costs?
  • How do I book follow-ups, and can I get test results by phone or email?

Choosing a vet for a senior cat

Older cats need a vet who knows them over time, because small changes in weight, drinking and behaviour are what point to early illness. Guidelines from the AAHA and AAFP recommend exams at least every six months for senior cats, and the AAFP recommends yearly blood and urine tests. Our senior cat health guide explains what a senior checkup includes and when to go straight in. If you are not sure whether your cat counts as a senior, read when a cat is considered senior.

It helps to stay with one clinic. A vet who has seen your cat's weight, test results and behaviour over several years notices change faster than one meeting your cat for the first time.

Plan for emergencies now

  • Find the nearest 24-hour emergency clinic using the map above, and save its number and address in your phone.
  • Keep your regular vet's number on the fridge and in your phone.
  • Know the signs that need urgent help. Difficulty breathing, collapse, repeated vomiting, not eating at all, and straining in the litter box with little or no urine all need a vet the same day. A male cat that cannot pass urine is an emergency. Our when to call the vet table lists the signs by urgency.
  • Phone ahead when you can. The clinic can tell you how to travel safely and prepare for your arrival.

Getting your cat ready for the visit

  • Use a sturdy carrier that opens from the top, and leave it out at home so your cat gets used to it.
  • Put a familiar blanket inside.
  • Bring your notes: weight, appetite, water, litter box changes and any photos or short videos of odd behaviour.
  • Bring past records if you are changing vets.
  • Feed your cat as usual unless the clinic tells you to withhold food.

If your cat gets very stressed by travel or handling, tell the clinic when you book. They can often offer a quieter slot or advice for easier visits.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find a good cat vet near me?

Search on a map, ask other cat owners, and read several recent reviews. Check the AAFP's Cat Friendly Practice search too, then phone or visit the clinic and ask the questions above before you book.

What is a cat-friendly vet?

A cat-friendly vet uses gentle handling, a calmer environment and equipment suited to cats. The AAFP's Cat Friendly Practice programme recognises clinics that take these extra steps.

Should I choose a vet before my cat gets ill?

Yes. Registering with a clinic early means your cat has a medical history on file, you know their hours, and you know where to go in an emergency. Choosing in a hurry usually means a worse choice.

How much does a cat vet visit cost?

Prices vary widely by country, clinic and treatment, so there is no reliable single figure. Ask for a written estimate before any test or treatment, and ask what is included.

Where should I take my cat after hours?

To a 24-hour emergency clinic. Find the nearest one in advance, save its number, and phone ahead if you can.
