Senior cat health

Cat Dementia
Signs.

Cat dementia, called feline cognitive dysfunction, is a gradual decline in memory, awareness and behaviour that can affect older cats. Typical signs are night-time yowling, getting lost in familiar rooms, and toilet accidents. Many of these signs can also come from pain or illness, so the first step is always a vet visit to rule out other causes.

Behaviour changes usually become noticeable in cats aged 10 and over, which is when vets count a cat as a senior (see when a cat is considered senior). This page explains the signs, how dementia differs from other age-related problems, how vets work it out, and what helps at home.

What is cat dementia?

Cornell's Feline Health Center describes cognitive dysfunction as a progressive condition with signs similar to Alzheimer's disease and senile dementia in people. Cats may show changes in memory, learning, awareness of their surroundings, sleep and how they interact with the household.

It is hard to put one number on how common it is. A review in Today's Veterinary Practice, citing a study by Gunn-Moore and colleagues, reported that 28% of cats aged 11 to 14 had changes in social interaction, and that 50% of cats over 15 showed aimless activity and excessive vocalisation. Those figures describe individual signs in older cats, not confirmed diagnoses, but they show that such changes are common in very old cats.

Signs of dementia in cats

VCA Animal Hospitals lists the signs in cats with the acronym VISHDAAL. Here they are in plain words, with what you might see:

AreaWhat you may see
VocalisingLoud, demanding or mournful calling, often at night
InteractionMore clingy, more distant or more irritable than before
Sleep and wakingSleeping more by day, then pacing and yowling at night
House soilingUrinating or passing stools outside the litter box
DisorientationStaring at walls or into space, getting stuck behind furniture, seeming lost in a familiar room

AnxietyEasily startled or fearful of ordinary noises
LearningSlow to respond to their name, or struggling with new routines

One or two of these signs, now and then, is not proof of dementia. A pattern that builds over weeks or months is what to report.

Dementia or something else?

Dementia is diagnosed by ruling other things out. Cornell notes that arthritis, kidney failure, hyperthyroidism, high blood pressure and gum disease can all cause similar changes. That is why a sudden or marked change should never be put down to "just old age".

How vets diagnose it

  1. History. Your vet asks what has changed, when it started and how it varies through the day. Notes, dates and short videos help a lot.
  2. Physical exam. Includes weight, joints, mouth, heart and often blood pressure.
  3. Tests. Today's Veterinary Practice lists a blood chemistry profile, a blood count, a thyroid hormone (T4) test, a urine test and blood pressure as a typical work-up to rule out other conditions.
  4. Diagnosis by exclusion. If nothing else explains the changes, dementia becomes the likely cause. VCA notes that definite confirmation requires examining brain tissue, which is only possible after death, so the diagnosis in a living cat is a clinical judgement.

VCA recommends annual screening for cognitive changes from age 10. If your cat has not had a senior check recently, our senior cat health guide explains what to ask for, and you can use our cat vet finder to find a clinic.

What you can do at home

There is no cure, but good routines and a cat-friendly home can make life calmer. These suggestions come from VCA and Cornell, and your vet can tailor them:

  • Keep the litter box easy. Use a large box with low sides and room to turn, and put one on every floor the cat uses.
  • Help with movement. Add ramps or steps to favourite spots, and put runners or mats on slippery floors.
  • Keep daily enrichment. Short play sessions, puzzle feeders and simple games keep the mind busy. Even moving a toy for your cat to track with their eyes counts.
  • Keep routines steady. Feed, play and rest at similar times each day, and avoid moving furniture or adding new pets.
  • Help if sight is fading. Extra lighting, such as a night light, can help your cat find their way.
  • Check the diet with your vet. Today's Veterinary Practice says no prescription feline diet exists to treat dementia, and Cornell suggests a senior diet rich in vitamin E and antioxidants. Make sure your cat actually likes the food. Our senior cat diet guide covers feeding in later life.

When your cat is up at night

Night-time calling is one of the most disruptive signs. Many owners find that a play session and a meal in the evening help, and a dim night light is worth trying, but ask your vet first, because night restlessness can also come from an overactive thyroid or pain. Do not punish a cat for yowling. It is usually distress or confusion.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment starts with a full medical and behavioural assessment. VCA says behavioural measures may need to continue even after other conditions are under control. There is no medicine licensed specifically for dementia in cats. Cornell mentions that a vet may prescribe an anti-anxiety drug for some signs, and VCA says research on supplements is limited. Never give your cat a supplement or any human or dog medicine without your vet's advice, because some ingredients that are safe for dogs are not safe for cats.

Living with an older cat with dementia

Cats with dementia can still enjoy life: warm spots, gentle company, favourite foods and quiet routines. Expect good days and bad days, and keep a short diary of sleep, appetite, toilet habits and mood so you and your vet can spot trends. Frequently asked questions

Can cats get dementia?

Yes. Cognitive dysfunction syndrome is the veterinary name. It mostly affects older cats, and signs usually become noticeable from around age 10.

At what age does cat dementia start?

Cornell says behaviour signs tend to appear in cats aged 10 or older. Some brain changes can begin earlier, but they do not always cause signs.

Why does my old cat yowl at night?

It can be dementia, but it can also be caused by an overactive thyroid, high blood pressure or pain, so a vet check comes first.

Is there a test for cat dementia?

No single test confirms it in a living cat. Vets run blood and urine tests and check blood pressure to rule out other causes, then judge from your cat's behaviour.

Can dementia be treated or cured?

It cannot be cured, but a calm routine, an easier home, enrichment and sometimes medical support can help. Your vet can advise on what suits your cat.

Is my cat in pain if they have dementia?

Not necessarily, but older cats often have pain from arthritis or dental disease as well, and pain can make confusion look worse. Ask your vet to check for it.

— This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If your cat seems unwell or the signs change suddenly, contact a vet.
