Senior cat health

Senior Cat
Health.

Senior cat health comes down to three habits: regular vet checkups, a short monthly check at home, and knowing which signs mean "call today". Cats hide discomfort well, so changes are usually small and slow. This guide shows what to look for, what to do about it, and where to read more on each common problem.

Veterinary guidelines count a cat as a mature adult from 7 to 10 years and a senior after 10 (see when a cat is considered senior). The advice below is aimed mainly at cats over 10, but starting the habits a little earlier costs nothing.

How often should a senior cat see the vet?

Twice a year is the usual advice. The AAHA/AAFP life stage guidelines recommend annual exams as a minimum for adult cats, increasing to every six months for seniors. Cornell's Feline Health Center gives the same twice-yearly advice for cats once they pass 10.

The AAFP's senior care brochure goes a step further: checkups every six months for cats aged 10 to 15, and every four months for cats over 15. Cats that already have a long-term condition may need to be seen more often, so follow your own vet's schedule.

What a senior checkup usually covers

  • Weight and body condition, compared with the last visit.
  • Mouth and teeth, because dental pain is common and often hidden.
  • Heart, lungs and abdomen, including feeling the kidneys and thyroid area.
  • Joints and movement, to look for stiffness or pain.
  • Blood pressure, eyes, skin and coat.
  • Blood and urine tests, which the AAFP recommends yearly so your vet can compare new results with old ones and spot change early.

How to get more from each visit

  • Bring your home notes (see below) and any photos or short videos of odd behaviour, since cats often act normal in the clinic.
  • Bring a fresh urine sample only if your vet asks you to.
  • Write your questions down before you go. Ask what is normal for your cat's age and what to re-check at the next visit.

A five-minute monthly check at home

Pick the same day each month. Keep notes in your phone: trends over weeks matter more than any single day.

  1. Weigh your cat. Use bathroom scales (weigh yourself holding the cat, then alone) or a baby scale. Write the number down. A change of even a few ounces can matter in a cat.
  2. Watch the water bowl. Is your cat drinking or urinating more than usual? Larger or more frequent litter clumps are a clue.
  3. Notice appetite. Eating more, eating less, or dropping food are all worth mentioning.
  4. Check the litter box. Accidents, straining, or avoiding the box can be early signs of pain or illness.
  5. Watch movement. Hesitating before a jump, skipping the stairs, or sleeping in new low spots can point to sore joints.
  6. Look at the mouth, coat and eyes. Bad breath, red gums, a greasy or unkempt coat, or cloudy eyes should be reported.
  7. Feel for lumps. Run your hands over the body and note any new swelling or skin mass.

The AAFP also reminds owners that any change in a cat's normal behaviour or routine can be a sign of pain, so "just getting old" is never the whole explanation.

A cat that stops eating can develop serious liver problems, so a missed meal or two is not something to watch for long. A male cat that strains to urinate and produces nothing is an emergency. If you are unsure which row your cat is in, phone the clinic: describing the signs takes two minutes and the staff can tell you how quickly to come in.

The five common problems in older cats

Cornell's Feline Health Center lists chronic kidney disease, diabetes, dental disease, hyperthyroidism and certain cancers as more common in older cats. Arthritis and weight changes are also very common. These five are the ones owners most often meet, and each has its own guide:

These conditions overlap. An overweight cat is more likely to develop arthritis, and a cat with dental pain may eat less and lose weight. Several of them share the same early signs (weight loss, thirst, appetite change), which is why a vet visit with tests is the only reliable way to tell them apart.

Older cats can also show confusion, night-time yowling or toilet accidents that are not caused by the body alone. Read our guide to cat dementia signs to see how it differs from pain and illness.

How care changes from 10 to 15 and beyond

Ages 10 to 14

This is when most cats move to twice-yearly checks and yearly blood and urine tests. Focus on keeping a healthy weight, good dental care and an easy-to-reach home: low-sided litter boxes, steps to favourite sofas and food and water on the same floor as the cat. The senior cat diet guide covers feeding at this stage.

Ages 15 and over

Very old cats change faster, which is why the AAFP suggests more frequent checks after 15. Expect more sleep, less grooming and more need for warmth and quiet. Keep your monthly notes, tell your vet about every change, and ask what your cat's quality of life looks like to them. Many cats live well past 15; our guide to how long cats live explains what the numbers really mean.

Making the home easier for an older cat

  • Put litter boxes with a low entry on every floor the cat uses.
  • Add a step or ramp to favourite high spots, and a warm, soft bed in a quiet place.
  • Keep food, water and the litter box close together and away from stairs.
  • Keep routines steady. Older cats cope best with the same feeding times and layout.
  • Brush gently and often if your cat can no longer groom well.

Breed and age

Some breeds carry extra risks. For example, kidney cysts are a known concern in Persians, as covered in our Persian lifespan guide. Frequently asked questions

What are the most common health problems in older cats?

Cornell's Feline Health Center lists chronic kidney disease, diabetes, dental disease, hyperthyroidism and certain cancers as more common in older cats. Arthritis and weight changes are also very common.

How often should a senior cat go to the vet?

Every six months is the standard advice for cats over 10. The AAFP suggests every four months for cats over 15, and more often for cats with an ongoing health problem.

How do I know if my senior cat is in pain?

Cats rarely cry out. More often they move less, jump less, groom less, hide, or change their litter box habits. The AAFP notes that any change in normal behaviour or routine can be a sign of pain, so raise it with your vet.

Should a senior cat have blood tests?

The AAFP recommends yearly blood and urine tests for senior cats, because they let your vet compare results over time. Some conditions, such as kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, can be found before obvious signs appear. Ask your vet what suits your cat.

Is it normal for an old cat to sleep more and play less?

Some slowing down is normal, but a sudden or large change is not something to put down to age alone. Pain, kidney problems and thyroid disease can all cause it. If the change is clear, book a checkup.

Can older cats still be healthy and happy?

Yes. Many cats live well into their teens with regular checks, a healthy weight, good dental care and a comfortable home.
