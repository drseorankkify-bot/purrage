Cat behaviour

Why Cats
Scratch.

Scratching is normal cat behaviour, not misbehaviour. Cats scratch to mark territory and to condition their claws. The fix is to give your cat something better to scratch, in the right place, rather than to try to stop the behaviour.

Scratching is natural cat behaviour, not bad behaviour. The way to protect your furniture is to give your cat something better to scratch, in the right place, rather than to try to stop it. This guide covers why cats scratch, how to choose and place a post, a step-by-step plan for redirecting furniture scratching, and what to do for older cats. See our cat behaviour guide for other habits.

Why cats scratch

  • Marking territory. Scratching leaves visible claw marks and scent from the foot pads.
  • Claw conditioning. It helps shorten and condition the claws.
  • Stretching and exercise. A long, full-body scratch is also a stretch, which is why a post needs to be tall enough.
  • Comfort. Anxious or afraid cats may scratch more, because marking territory is a reassuring and soothing activity for them.

Because scratching serves several purposes at once, a cat that is stopped from doing it without an alternative will usually find another surface. That is why redirecting works better than discouraging: the need stays the same, and only the target changes. A cat may also scratch more in the places it uses most, such as near where it sleeps, and just after waking, when stretching comes naturally.

Choosing a scratching post

Purina says material is the most important factor and that the best option varies between cats, so some trial and error is normal. A post can be simple or elaborate, as long as the cat uses it.

Size, stability and style

  • Height. Choose a post taller than your cat's body length so it can stretch upward while scratching. A short post limits the stretch and can lose the cat's interest.
  • Stability. Use a sturdy base so the post will not tip over when your cat is on it. Attach cat trees to the wall for stability.
  • Shape. Options include simple vertical posts on solid wooden bases, posts with horizontal platforms, wall-mounted frames at a height your cat can reach, and cat trees. Try horizontal as well as vertical surfaces and different shapes.
  • Replacement. Replace a post once the material is shredded and no longer satisfying. Sisal and carpet can often be renewed by replacing only the covering on the wooden post, while cardboard needs frequent replacement.

Where to put it

Placement is as important as the post itself.

  • Next to the problem spot. Place a post near, or on top of, a spot where your cat already scratches, to redirect scratching away from furniture and carpets.
  • Where your cat spends time. Good locations include near the bed, the litter tray or the sofa. Choose a central spot where the family spends the most time, not an unused corner.
  • Several posts. Have more than one around the home and check them regularly.
  • Away from hazards. Keep posts away from sharp corners such as counter or table edges that could injure a cat that climbs or jumps.
  • Make the area inviting. Hiding spots, toys or elevated perches nearby help.

Quick checklist before you buy

  • Taller than your cat's body length, with a sturdy base.
  • A material your cat is likely to like. Try sisal first, and be ready to try another.
  • More than one post, in the rooms your cat uses most.
  • Room to place it against the furniture your cat already scratches.
  • A replaceable covering, so you can renew it instead of buying a new post.

Cat trees combine scratching with climbing, jumping and resting, and let a cat survey its territory, so they suit homes with space. For older cats, a lower or horizontal scratcher may be easier to use.

What not to do

The sources we read focus on redirection and changes to the environment rather than punishment. Scratching is natural, so the goal is to channel it. Do not declaw: PetMD describes declawing as painful and says it can cause lameness and permanent nerve damage. If scratching is hard to control, talk to your vet, who can check for stress or medical causes.

Scratching and older cats

If an older cat scratches less or stops using a tall post, mention it to your vet, since pain such as arthritis can change how cats move. Horizontal or lower surfaces are worth trying, and the sources suggest offering a mix of shapes. See our senior cat home setup guide for ways to make the home easier to get around. Keeping nails trimmed also helps; see senior cat grooming.

When scratching signals stress

Scratching can also increase when a cat is anxious, since marking territory is soothing. If scratching rises after a move, a new pet or a change in routine, check what has changed at home and give your cat more secure places to retreat. Read cat body language for other signs of stress, and ask your vet if the behaviour persists.

Frequently asked questions

Why do cats scratch furniture?

Scratching is normal behaviour. Cats scratch to mark territory, with visible claw marks and scent from their foot pads, and to shorten and condition their claws.

How do I get my cat to use a scratching post?

Put the post directly against the furniture your cat scratches, reward your cat when it uses it, then move it gradually to its permanent spot. Make it tall enough for a full stretch and try different textures and shapes.

What is the best material for a scratching post?

It varies by cat. Sisal fabric allows uninterrupted strokes and sisal rope gives a good grip. Carpet is popular but may teach cats to scratch carpets elsewhere, and cardboard is cheap but less durable. Expect some trial and error.

Where should I put a scratching post?

Near or on top of a spot where your cat already scratches, and in a central place where the family spends time, for example near the bed, litter tray or sofa. Not in an unused corner.

How tall should a scratching post be?

Taller than your cat's body length so it can stretch upward while scratching, with a sturdy base that will not tip.

Do anxious cats scratch more?

They can. Marking territory is a reassuring and soothing activity for anxious or afraid cats.

Should I declaw my cat to protect furniture?

No. PetMD describes declawing as painful and says it can cause lameness and permanent nerve damage. Redirect scratching and keep claws trimmed instead.

— This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If your cat seems unwell, contact a vet.
